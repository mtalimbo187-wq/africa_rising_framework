## 📝 COMPLETE NARRATION SCRIPT

### SECTION 1: THE HOOK (45-60 seconds)
**Timecode: 0:00-0:50**

```
In 2023, one man in Lagos completed a project that was supposed to be 
impossible. 

A single refinery that processes more crude oil than most African nations 
produce in a year.

650,000 barrels per day.

Built by an African. Funded by Africans. Owned by an African.

For 70 years, Africa pumped oil out of the ground, shipped it raw to Europe 
and America, and bought it back refined — paying twice.

One man ended that deal.
```

---

### SECTION 2: CONTEXT (55-70 seconds)
**Timecode: 0:50-2:00**

```
Nigeria is Africa's second-largest economy. 

It has the continent's largest proven oil reserves: 37 billion barrels.

Yet Nigeria imports 90 percent of the refined fuel it uses.

Why?

Because in the 1980s, structural adjustment programs from the IMF forced 
Nigeria to sell its refineries to private buyers. Those buyers stripped them 
for parts or abandoned them.

By 2015, Nigeria — an oil superpower — couldn't refine its own fuel.

It bought gasoline from the Netherlands.

That's the equivalent of Saudi Arabia importing oil from Canada. It's 
backwards. It's by design.
```

---

### SECTION 3: THE CORE STORY (60-75 seconds)
**Timecode: 2:00-3:15**

```
Aliko Dangote is worth 12 billion dollars.

He made his fortune in cement. In 2006, at age 49, he announced he would 
build a refinery.

Everyone laughed.

"Nigeria doesn't have the infrastructure," they said.
"The government won't allow it," they said.
"You need 15 years and you'll go bankrupt," they said.

He took 17 years.
He spent 20 billion dollars.

He moved 3,000 tons of equipment through Lagos port — some pieces so large 
they required specially designed ships.

He hired 3,000 workers, trained them from zero to refinery-grade skill level.

He navigated Nigerian politics, land disputes, and bureaucracy that would 
have destroyed a lesser project.

In May 2023, the refinery began operations.

The first African-owned, full-scale oil refinery of the modern era was born.
```

---

### SECTION 4: THE HUMAN COST (55-70 seconds)
**Timecode: 3:15-4:25**

```
3,000 workers were trained to operate a facility that didn't exist 
beforehand.

In a country where unemployment is 33 percent, this matters. It matters 
deeply.

But these workers also faced conditions: 12-hour shifts, industrial hazard 
exposure, relocation away from their communities, risk.

Engineer Chioma worked on the project for eight years. She was one of 
200 female engineers on the construction site — unusual for Nigerian oil 
and gas.

"We knew what we were building," she says in interviews. "But we also knew 
the pressure. One mistake and thousands of jobs disappear. One accident and 
Lagos remembers why refineries are dangerous."

The refinery sits 30 kilometers from Lagos, a megacity of 15 million people.

An industrial accident here wouldn't just affect the plant. It would affect 
millions.

The workers carried that weight.
```

---

### SECTION 5: THE SYSTEM (60-70 seconds)
**Timecode: 4:25-5:40**

```
Why did the IMF force Nigeria to sell its refineries in the 1980s?

Structural adjustment. The theory was simple: privatization creates 
efficiency.

The reality was different: Private buyers didn't invest in upkeep. They 
extracted wealth and left the shells to rust.

For 40 years, this extracted wealth from Nigeria. Every naira spent on 
imported fuel was money leaving the country, never coming back.

The refinery changes this equation.

But Dangote didn't build this alone. He needed political capital. He had to 
negotiate with the federal government for crude oil supply contracts. He had 
to negotiate with Lagos State for land and environmental permits. He had to 
negotiate with the Nigerian military for security.

This is not just engineering. This is politics disguised as infrastructure.

Dangote served as informal advisor to three presidents. He funded campaigns. 
He has relationships with governors that transcend business.

The refinery was possible because Dangote had power.

Not everyone has that power.
```

---

### SECTION 6: THE SCALE (55-65 seconds)
**Timecode: 5:40-6:45**

```
650,000 barrels per day.

To understand what that means: Nigeria currently produces 1.7 million 
barrels per day.

The Dangote Refinery will process 38 percent of Nigeria's total crude 
production.

That oil no longer gets exported as crude. It comes back as refined fuel: 
gasoline, diesel, jet fuel, kerosene.

The economic model is simple but powerful: Instead of selling crude at 
$50 per barrel, Nigeria keeps the crude and sells refined fuel at $80 to 
$90 per barrel equivalent value.

The refining margin — the difference — stays in Nigeria.

On full capacity, this is worth $8 to $10 billion per year in refining 
revenue.

That's 2 to 3 percent of Nigeria's entire GDP coming from one facility.

It's also a security play: Nigeria is less dependent on imports if refining 
capacity is domestic.
```

---

### SECTION 7: THE RESPONSE (50-65 seconds)
**Timecode: 6:45-7:50**

```
When the refinery opened in 2023, the Nigerian government celebrated.

President Tinubu called it "a watershed moment for Nigeria's energy 
independence."

But the response has been complicated.

Within weeks, fuel prices in Nigeria didn't drop as promised. Why?

The refinery supplies the export market first — higher margins. Domestic 
fuel comes second.

This created political pressure: Nigerians watched the refinery produce fuel 
while they still waited in line at gas stations.

Additionally, Dangote demanded that Nigeria stop importing refined fuel and 
force all refiners to use his facility. This triggered antitrust concerns 
from international observers.

The Western response was muted. Quiet.

Why? Because the refinery threatens a 70-year business model where Africa 
stays dependent on imported energy.
```

---

### SECTION 8: THE MEANING (50-65 seconds)
**Timecode: 7:50-8:30**

```
The Dangote Refinery is not the story of one man's wealth.

It's the story of what African independence looks like when executed at 
scale.

Not waiting for aid. Not waiting for investment from the West.

One African building infrastructure that fundamentally changes Africa's 
position in the global economy.

But it also reveals the limits: This wasn't possible for most people. It 
required $20 billion in capital. It required direct relationships with 
presidents and generals. It required 17 years of patience and being 
already-wealthy enough to absorb losses.

The refinery proves Africa can do this.

It doesn't solve the structural inequality that made refineries necessary in 
the first place.

The next question is: Who comes next?

Can others replicate this without needing to be a billionaire?

That's the story we're watching now.
```
